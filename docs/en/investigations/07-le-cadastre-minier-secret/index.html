I. The Concealed History of the Corsican Subsoil : From Antimony to Rare Earths

In common perception, Corsica is viewed exclusively as a mountainous tourism destination or pastoral landscape. However, geological history reveals an industrial past of strategic importance. From the 19th to the mid-20th century, the island subsoil underwent extensive mineral extraction: the antimony mines of Cap Corse (Meria, Luri, Ersa), the copper and pyrite deposits of Ponte-Leccia (Castifao, Moltifao), iron deposits in Farinole, and the large-scale asbestos quarry of Canari.

While these operations were abruptly shuttered due to short-term financial shifts, contemporary global tensions over critical raw materials essential for energy transition (antimony for military defense and batteries, copper, rare earth elements) have reignited the interest of central state geological agencies and multinational mining groups.

II. Central State Strategy and the IRM 2024 Mineral Resources Inventory

Under the French Mining Code (Code Minier), the subsoil belongs exclusively to the state rather than surface landowners. The French government retains the unilateral authority to grant Exclusive Exploration Permits (PER) and mining concessions without prior consent from municipal councils or the Assembly of Corsica.

Analysis of the Bureau of Geological and Mining Research (BRGM) database and Energy-Mining GIS maps reveals that a quiet update of the Corsican Mining Inventory was initiated by the central Ministry. Explorations target rare earth element concentrations in interior granitic complexes and reevaluate high-grade antimony veins in Cap Corse.

« The French Mining Code permits the central state to issue exploration and mining concessions across Corsican soil via simple Council of State decrees, completely bypassing the regional PADDUC sustainable development plan. »

III. The Unaddressed Environmental Liabilities : Meria and Canari Slag Heaps

Before any new mining ventures are contemplated, L’OCHJU’s environmental audit exposes the state’s abandonment of historical mining wastelands. At the former Meria antimony mine and the Canari asbestos site, thousands of tons of heavy metal slag and tailings (arsenic, antimony, lead) continue to erode into local streams and the sea during torrential rainstorms.

Public health requests by local mayors demanding comprehensive soil decontamination have been met with state inaction, citing ancient concession expirations to evade liability.

VI. Forensic Analysis of Administrative Decrees & Mining Jurisprudence

Scrutiny of mining concession decrees and environmental enforcement reveals profound statutory discrepancies:

  1. Analysis of State Exploration Concessions : Central decrees bypass regional land planning laws by categorizing mineral exploration as « national strategic defense priorities ».
  2. Scrutiny of Environmental Inaction : State agencies systematically classify toxic runoff at historic mine sites as « diffuse historical pollution », evading polluter-pays mandates under EU Directive 2004/35/EC.
  3. Audit of Regional Subsoil Competencies : The Corsican Special Statute lacks legislative control over the subsoil, maintaining central state ownership over island mineral commons.

VII. Citizen FOIA (CADA) Recourse Framework for Subsurface Transparency

To prevent covert exploration and force environmental remediation, Article L. 311-1 CRPA enables citizens to demand:

  1. Certified Geochemical Soil Assay Sheets (BRGM / DREAL) : Access to raw laboratory sampling data for watersheds adjacent to historic mining perimeters.
  2. State Concession Grant Files (Ministry of Economy) : Comprehensive administrative files for any active or pending Exclusive Exploration Permits.
  3. Water Basin Heavy Metal Contamination Ledgers (Water Agency) : Testing data for municipal tap water sources downstream from old mining works.
  4. PADDUC Environmental Alignment Audits (CdC) : Regional reviews assessing conflicts between mining exploration perimeters and protected nature reserves.

  • Collectivité de Corse (CdC) : Manages surface territorial planning (PADDUC) but lacks statutory ownership of the subsoil.
