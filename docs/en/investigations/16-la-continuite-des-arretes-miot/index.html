I. The History of the Miot Decrees : A Century-Old Fiscal Safeguard

In June 1798 (Year VI of the French Republican calendar), the General Administrator of Corsica, André-François Miot de Melito, enacted a set of fiscal decrees adapting inheritance taxation to the island’s unique family land tenure. The Miot Decrees granted an exemption from inheritance taxes on real estate located in Corsica and drastically reduced duties on property divisions and sales.

For over two centuries, this fiscal exception allowed Corsican families to retain ancestral village homes, olive groves, and chestnut orchards without being forced into distress public auctions to pay prohibitive inheritance taxes.

II. Progressive Dismantling and the Chronic Indivision Trap

Under continuous pressure from the French Treasury (Bercy) and constitutional court rulings in the name of fiscal uniformity, successive governments initiated the phased elimination of the Miot Decrees. Now subjected to a rigid phase-out schedule, Corsican heirs face devastating tax liabilities upon succession.

Compounding this fiscal pressure is the widespread scourge of land indivision (l’indivision foncière). Having omitted formal notarized deeds of estate division across multiple generations (often since the 19th century), tens of thousands of parcels in Corsica are co-owned by dozens, sometimes hundreds, of dispersed co-heirs. Legally paralyzed, these lands cannot be maintained, farmed by local youth, or sold to resident buyers.

« The lack of clear, validated property titles affects over 40% of private land area in Corsica. This cadastral disorder provides an open gateway for external developers who buy out isolated co-heirs to force judicial auction sales (licitation). »

III. The Role of GIRTEC and the Risk of Forced Auction Sales

To address this unprecedented cadastral deadlock, the French state and Collectivité de Corse established the GIRTEC (Public Interest Group for the Reconstitution of Property Titles in Corsica). Utilizing genealogists and surveyors, GIRTEC assists families in establishing acquisitive prescription notoriety deeds (30-year adverse possession / usucapion).

However, GIRTEC’s annual operational budget remains chronically inadequate relative to the 150,000 untitled parcels across the island. Meanwhile, speculative wealth funds exploit Article 815 of the French Civil Code (« No one can be compelled to remain in indivision ») to buy minor shares from distant diaspora relatives and trigger forced judicial auctions of entire family estates.

VI. Forensic Analysis of Administrative Decrees & Succession Jurisprudence

Scrutiny of succession administrative directives reveals critical statutory issues:

  1. Analysis of Article 750 bis A CGI Expiration Schedules : The rigid transition timetable threatens thousands of modest rural families with forced liquidation of ancestral assets.
  2. Scrutiny of Judicial Auction Procedures : Civil courts systematically permit external buyers to force entire estate auctions without granting municipal preemption rights.
  3. Audit of Adverse Possession (Usucapion) Notarial Costs : Administrative fees for notoriety deeds remain prohibitive for low-income resident heirs.

VII. Citizen FOIA (CADA) Recourse Framework for Succession Security

Under Article L. 311-1 CRPA, citizens can demand:

  1. Certified GIRTEC Municipal Intervention Sheets (GIRTEC) : Village-by-village title reconstitution status.

  3. Court of Appeal Judicial Property Auction Records (Courts) : Sale prices and acquirer identities in forced estate liquidations.
  4. State Inheritance Exemption Extension Decrees (Bercy) : Legislative evaluations on island property tax continuity.
